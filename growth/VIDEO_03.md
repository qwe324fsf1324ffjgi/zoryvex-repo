# VIDEO 03 — "Why your certificate might not count"

**Genuinely useful to a candidate even if they never sign up.** This is the
teaching video.

- **Length:** 30s · **Format:** talking head or voice-over screen · **One idea:** a certificate counts when it is bound to you at the issuer
- **Claimable:** yes, with care — see below

## Hook — second zero

Caption over a certificate image: **"Employers stopped caring about this. Here's what changed."**

## Script

> "A certificate used to be enough on its own. It isn't now, and the reason
> is simple: a lot of them can be finished in a weekend, and a PDF of one can
> be made in a minute.
>
> So the question stopped being *do you have it* and became *can anyone
> confirm it*.
>
> A certificate counts when the body that issued it publishes a record bound
> to you. You give the verification address, the issuer confirms the name and
> the credential, and nobody has to take your word for it.
>
> That works for AWS. It works for Google Cloud. It works for the Linux
> Foundation's Kubernetes exams, and for IBM's HashiCorp certifications.
>
> It does not work for Microsoft or Azure. Microsoft left Credly in 2024 and
> Microsoft Learn doesn't publish a credential bound to the holder's address.
> There's nothing to check. That isn't a gap in a tool. There's just no source.
>
> If you're choosing what to study next and you want it to be checkable, that
> difference is worth knowing."

## Claim discipline

- The four verifiable issuers are exactly what the product supports. Accurate.
- The Microsoft statement is a documented product constraint, stated
  factually. **Do not editorialise it into a swipe at Microsoft.** Say what is
  true: there is no address to check.
- Do not say "most certificates are worthless". Do not imply a weekend
  certificate means a weak candidate.
